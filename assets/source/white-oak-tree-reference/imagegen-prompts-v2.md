# White Oak Tree Reference, Version 2

Both sheets were made with the built-in ImageGen tool. The user's photograph guided the trunk proportions, crown shape, irregular foliage distribution, and daylight. The generated summer sheet served as the edit target for the bare structure.

The views run left to right from front to three-quarter to side. The angles and hidden branch structure are artist approximations.

## Summer foliage generation prompt

Create a new photorealistic full-summer white oak tree reference sheet for a tree artist, inspired by Image 1. Image 1 is field-photography reference for the old tree's silhouette, low trunk fork, the way growth occupies space, fine foliage texture, and outdoor light. Do not reproduce the location or its people, signs, power lines or houses. The new tree is an old, open-grown eastern white oak (Quercus alba) with more foliage coverage and greater crown depth than shown in the photo.

Layout: three full-tree field photographs of ONE consistent tree, from three different sides, in one very wide landscape sheet (target 3:1). Left shows the selected front inspired by the photo, middle is camera rotated about 45 degrees, right is camera rotated about 90 degrees. Individually center each tree in its third, each crown fully visible with a clear gutter, all roots and tops visible, same ground/camera height and equal scale. Actual camera rotations with visible differences in projected limb arrangement and silhouette. Almost-level natural eye-height perspective from enough distance to see the whole tree with minimal lens distortion. All three views use the same quiet, pale daylight sky and low grassy ground with soft distant indistinct greenery and a shared horizon at the bottom, no scene clutter. Natural late morning sunlight with bright leaf tips, reflected cool light in shadow, warm-green translucency where thin leaf edges catch the sun, soft sky fill, deep shade concentrated under the canopy. Faithful outdoor botanical photography, natural color and fine organic variation; no split-panel borders, captions, graphic elements, or wide blank margins.

Structure and shape: Use one mature, rough gray-brown trunk, wide at its root flare, narrower above, uninterrupted at soil level. Low, irregular strong forks separate into several unequal scaffold limbs, dividing at staggered heights and opening out in three dimensions. Give lower boughs lateral reach, one slightly dipping low bough on the left and a steeper higher limb on the right in front view, plus an offset high crown and a few lower asymmetric draping ends. Tie these landmarks together across camera rotations. Show a broad, generous and deep crown about 30–40 percent wider than the total tree height in the front view. Foliage descends irregularly low around a short exposed trunk, as in the field photo. Branch lengths, bends, forks and mass are varied, curves interrupted by gradual changes of direction, visible limbs taper, and bark forks merge as living wood. No long repetitive upturned hooks, no orderly levels of branches, no second trunk from the ground, no bonsai, no pruned park-tree geometry.

Foliage is the most important change from the source photo: close up the spaces across the mid-crown and especially over the trunk and main branches in the center of the canopy. In every view, a screen of overlapping foliage at different depths hides the inner scaffold and all long paths of mid-crown wood. Leave the root flare and lower bole and the start of the fork visible beneath the leaf curtain. Let only a few uneven small holes occur between volumes, mainly near the outside and near the ends of lower boughs. No prominent holes in the middle third of the crown. Keep the natural irregular spacing of the real tree: some neighbouring masses meet, others overlap, some tiny recessed pockets deepen toward shadow, and the actual branch-fed patches differ substantially in depth, scale and shape. Make the outer profile irregular like a real open-grown oak, with deep asymmetric shoulders, changing heights, small loose frayed edges and local pendulous sprays. No repeating stacked sheets, even radial puffs, detached balls, ornamental cloud-pruning, smooth outer dome, bare twig tips, or smooth solid foliage shell. The leaves are small at this whole-tree scale, many thousands across the crown. Give the fine-grained surface the irregular detail of individual naturally curled leaves with rounded lobes, not oversized individual leaf shapes, thick waxy outlines or flat dark patches. Darker foliage is still foliage. Preserve the natural wood appearance and quiet field-photo finish. Output a credible reference a tree artist could use to approve the canopy design.

## Bare structure edit prompt

Make a leafless artist reference sheet by editing the exact supplied three-view summer white oak turnaround. The supplied turnaround is the edit target for matching identity, composition, and woody structure. Use it as an image edit, in a single image. A contemporary botanical photograph in the same quiet outdoor field.

Invariants: Keep the three full trees at exactly the same horizontal and vertical positions, view angles, camera height, scale, daylight, and 3:1 aspect ratio as the input. Same front, three-quarter and side views left to right. Match the exact root flare, bark character, low basal forks, and everything visible of the major limbs in each view. Keep each whole tree within its frame above the same ground contact line. Keep the sky, grassy field, and low distant background in place. Adjust only each tree and its cast shadow as appropriate for a leafless tree. No labels or dividers. No snow or winter fog, no dead tree ambiance.

Defoliate these trees completely and render the now-visible woody architecture. This is a single mature white oak from three angles. It has a very substantial low bole with a flared root collar and an asymmetric, low division above ground into a few unequal ascending and spreading scaffolds. Those carry deep staggered forks and numerous tapered branches emerging forward, backward, laterally and obliquely in depth. Preserve the current thick trunk and its first distinctive fork in each view. The multiple low axes are continuations of that single trunk, combining lateral limbs and several oblique forks. Outer boughs extend then shallowly dip or sweep at different pitches. The longest drooping sprays extend low toward the ground on different sides. Off-center top has a modest ragged crest. Length, angle and spacing change from limb to limb; avoid repetitions. Low branch connections must look like grown wood with continuous collars and gray-brown ridged scales. Show clear branch taper down through the hierarchy to the smallest fine twigs; let shading and occlusion make the foreground branches distinguishable.

Reconstruct where woody shoots would actually be needed to carry this specific tree's deep irregular foliage in the target. Extend the branches into the same big lobes, hanging shapes and asymmetrical silhouette seen in every summer view, with fine wood reaching almost to their margins. Use many varied networks of thin tertiary branches and terminal twig forks, clustered irregularly in the front, across the center and at the back of the crown. Forks originate along parent limbs as well as close to the tips. Each network fills a separate three-dimensional region that projects over other regions, with clusters of short shoots beside diffuse long ones and variable small open spaces among them. Branching masses should relate across views, with low boughs shortening in projection as they point toward the camera and groups of twigs showing up in changed positions across views for a real change in camera azimuth. Oak-like stepped turns, moderate curvature, natural fine twig texture and branch variation. No leaves, no clinging brown foliage, no fake thread haze, no replicated lobes or impenetrable black web. No long slick upward-hooked poles, simplified bare fingers, radial stars, even horizontal shelves, or isolated twig tufts. Show a convincing live dormant oak with enough small woody growth to support all of the summer density.
